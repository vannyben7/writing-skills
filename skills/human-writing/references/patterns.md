# Pattern reference: 35 legacy IDs

These IDs preserve compatibility with the supplied Humanizer 2.11.2 catalog. They
describe possible editing problems, not a detector. Check the paragraph's purpose
and meaning before acting. Do not count isolated words, grammar, punctuation, or
formal/non-native prose as evidence of AI authorship. A cluster can direct attention;
it still does not establish authorship or require a rewrite.

The examples illustrate editorial choices, not verified background information.
No example authorizes adding or dropping a substantive claim. The entrypoint's
preservation rules govern every pattern. Keep prose unchanged when it already works.

Contents: 1–6 content; 7–13 language; 14–19 typography; 20–22 chat residue;
23–25 filler/qualification; 26–35 further style and argument patterns.

### 1. Inflated claims about importance and legacy

Watch ceremonial significance attached to ordinary facts: “pivotal”, “a testament”,
“具有重要意义”. If it substitutes for explanation, reduce the ceremony. Preserve
the author's actual evaluation or a sourced historical interpretation. Do not turn
an explicit claim of significance into its opposite, or assume it has been disproved.

### 2. Name-dropping to prove importance

Publication lists and follower counts do not explain a person's ideas, but they are
still information. Keep all names and quantities unless summarization is requested.

Before: “Her views have been cited in The New York Times, BBC, Financial Times, and
The Hindu. She maintains an active social media presence with over 500,000 followers.”

After: “Her views have been cited in The New York Times, BBC, Financial Times, and
The Hindu. She has over 500,000 social media followers.”

Do not invent what the outlets reported to make the list more informative.

### 3. Shallow analysis with participial phrases

Chains of “highlighting”, “reflecting”, or “symbolizing” can conceal the relation
between observation and interpretation. Make an existing relation explicit or split
the clauses. Retain a real interpretation and its attribution; do not automatically
delete every `-ing` clause or treat inferred symbolism as physical fact.

### 4. Sales language

Replace redundant promotional framing when the genre calls for description.
Preserve actual recommendations, subjective experiences, and quoted promotion.
“Breathtaking” in a personal diary may express the author's response. Do not replace
it with a travel fact, an invented landscape, or a neutral opinion they did not hold.

### 5. Vague sources

“Experts believe” and “研究表明” require attention when their source is missing.
If the text supplies a named source, use it without changing attribution or certainty.
If not, retain the claim and its attributed status, then flag the missing source
outside the revised prose. Unverified does not mean false or expendable.

Before: “Experts believe the river plays an important role in the regional ecosystem.”

After: unchanged, with a separate note that the experts/source are not identified.
Do not invent researchers, convert belief to fact, or quietly delete the sentence.

### 6. Formulaic challenges and outlook sections

Repeated “despite these challenges” can flatten an argument. Reorganize the concrete
prosperity, problems, actions, and outlook actually stated. Keep each substantive
claim and its certainty. Do not add dates, initiatives, “recurring” problems, or a
positive forecast. A required risks section is not filler merely because it is regular.

### 7. Stock vocabulary

Possible clusters include “delve”, “tapestry”, “pivotal”, “showcase”, “underscore”,
“landscape”, and “此外……赋能……多维度……”. Edit for a specific reading problem,
not a banned-word list. Keep technical meanings, quotations, and natural isolated uses.

Before: “Additionally, Somali cuisine incorporates camel meat. Pasta dishes were
introduced during Italian colonization and remain widespread in the traditional diet.”

After: “Somali cuisine also includes camel meat. Pasta dishes, introduced during
Italian colonization, remain widespread in the traditional diet.”

“Considered a delicacy” and “especially in the south” were absent; neither may be added.

### 8. Avoiding is, are, and has

Prefer a simple copula when it expresses the same relation. “Represents” can have a
real theoretical meaning and “serves as” can describe a function; preserve that meaning.

Before: “Gallery 825 serves as LAAA's exhibition space for contemporary art. The
gallery features four separate spaces and boasts over 3,000 square feet.”

After: “Gallery 825 is LAAA's exhibition space for contemporary art. The gallery
has four separate spaces and over 3,000 square feet.”

Keep “over” and “spaces”; do not invent exact area or four enclosed rooms.

### 9. Formulaic contrasts and clipped negative endings

Check whether both halves of “not X but Y”, “并非……而是……”, or “not only” contribute
information. Retain a correction, mechanism contrast, negative result, or exclusion.
When a tail such as “no guessing” has an ambiguous actor, do not invent “the user”
or “the system”. Keep it or flag the ambiguity before changing its meaning.

Keep: “The estimate is imprecise, not evidence of no effect.”

### 10. Forced groups of three

Develop the number of points the source needs. Three genuine categories are fine.
Do not remove a category, change ranking, or redistribute simultaneous processes
into a timeline to make the rhythm less regular.

Before: “The event features keynote sessions, panel discussions, and networking.”
After, if a plainer register is desired: “The event includes keynotes, panels, and networking.”
Do not add that networking occurs between sessions.

### 11. Changing names and repeated openings

Keep one precise referent instead of cycling through near-synonyms. Join repeated
openings when that helps their relation; preserve anaphora or a deliberate refrain.
“She noted the door. She noted the lock on it.” may become “She noted the door and
its lock.” Do not merge entities that only appear to share a referent.

### 12. False ranges

A figurative “from X to Y” can be intentional. If it obscures a list, make the list
explicit without losing endpoints or items. Never change real dates, numeric ranges,
page spans, or ordered processes. This legacy ID is a clarity check, not an authorship tell.

### 13. Passive voice and missing subjects

Passive voice is often appropriate when the result or process is the topic. Keep
“Samples were stored at −20 °C.” If an actor matters and the source identifies it,
name it. If no actor is supplied, do not invent one. Chinese subject omission may
be entirely natural. “No configuration file is needed” can remain unchanged.

### 14. Dashes and ranges

Edit repetitive parenthetical interruptions only when they obscure emphasis. There
is no blanket ban on em dashes (—), en dashes (–), or Chinese dashes (——). Preserve
en dashes in ranges such as “2019–2021” and “pp. 3–7”, minus signs in values, lexical
hyphens, and every mark inside code, flags, URLs, titles, citations, or locked spans.
Do not reproduce a sample's punctuation frequency mechanically.

### 15. Too much bold text

Remove decorative emphasis when prose makes the hierarchy clear. Preserve semantic
formatting, interface labels, assignment requirements, accessibility needs, and
requested Markdown. Do not remove definitions while removing bold markers.

### 16. Lists with bold mini-headings

A list can be the right format for parallel items. Convert it to connected prose
when the argument depends on progression, keeping all items and their relations.
Do not translate “optimized algorithms” into “faster page loading” unless stated.

### 17. Title case in headings

Follow the author's or target publication's heading convention. Sentence case is
an option, not a universal correction. Preserve exact titles and proper names.

### 18. Emojis

Remove unnecessary decoration only if it conflicts with the genre or requested tone.
An emoji may be an intentional personal signal. “Users prefer simplicity” must not
be rewritten as “User research showed...” without an actual study in the source.

### 19. Quotation typography

Follow the format's typography; curly quotes and Chinese quotation marks are normal.
Do not treat them as evidence of authorship. Exact quotations and program strings
are protected, even if surrounding prose uses another convention.

### 20. Chatbot residue

Remove greetings and offers accidentally left in a standalone article. Do not fill
the resulting gap with background facts the input never gave.

Before: “Here is an overview: the committee met in May. I hope this helps!”
After: “The committee met in May.”

### 21. Knowledge disclaimers and speculative gap-filling

Remove assistant self-reference only when it is not part of the user's intended
content. Keep meaningful “as of” dates and limits on the evidence. A speculative
founding date remains speculative; lack of a citation alone does not authorize its
deletion. Flag unsupported guesses separately or seek source verification when
requested. Do not turn “not found in these materials” into “not publicly available”.

### 22. Reflexive agreement

Remove generic praise left around the answer when it adds no intended content.
Preserve the author's genuine agreement or disagreement. “Great question!” can be
cut from a pasted article; it does not license inventing the answer that follows.

### 23. Filler phrases

“In order to” may become “to”; “由于……的原因” may become “由于……”. Keep a phrase if
it states an actual temporal, conditional, causal, or contrastive relation. Avoid
automatic replacement where the shorter form changes scope.

### 24. Stacked qualifiers

Reduce only qualifiers that duplicate the same uncertainty. “Could potentially
possibly affect outcomes” can become “may affect outcomes”. Preserve independent
limits, such as “may apply only to the urban subgroup”, and evidential distinctions
such as “suggests” versus “demonstrates”. Never equate confidence with good voice.

### 25. Generic positive endings

Cut a redundant send-off only if it adds no claim or deliberate emotional closure.
Keep a real forecast, plan, recommendation, open question, or personal feeling.
End where the supplied reasoning arrives; do not add a grand implication for closure.

### 26. Hyphenated compounds

Follow lexical meaning and the applicable convention. Do not mechanically strip
hyphens after verbs: “cross-functional”, “well-known”, and “end-to-end” may retain
them. Compound modifiers, negative numbers, CLI flags, and mathematical operators
have different functions. Never alter code to satisfy a prose preference.

### 27. Claims of a deeper truth

Examine “at its core”, “the real question”, and “本质上”. Remove repeated staging
when the point is already clear. Keep a real conceptual distinction. Do not turn
“organizational readiness matters” into a new causal claim about changed habits.

### 28. Announcing the next point

Trim a redundant announcement when the substantive statement already follows.
Keep signposting that helps a complex argument or instruction.

Before: “Let's examine the cache: it stores responses for five minutes.”
After: “The cache stores responses for five minutes.”

### 29. Repeating a heading

Remove an opening sentence that only duplicates the heading. Keep a definition,
scope limit, or orienting claim even if it shares the heading's words.

### 30. Narrating a previous version

Describe current behavior when that is the document's purpose, but keep relevant
history, comparisons, and migration guidance. A sentence about replacing quadratic
iteration does not establish that a hash map is now used or that lookups are O(1).
If the source gives no current implementation, do not invent one to replace history.

### 31. Forced punchlines and fragments

Reduce repeated dramatic closers when they obscure the reasoning. A short sentence
or an intentional fragment may carry real emphasis. Do not impose minimum length,
add a long sentence for balance, or invent a causal explanation when joining fragments.

### 32. Formulaic sayings

An aphorism may be the author's considered expression. If “X is the language of Y”
is vague and no concrete claim is supplied, preserve it or flag the missing meaning.
Do not invent a user study, predictability mechanism, or workflow effect to explain it.

### 33. Staged candor

Repeated “Honestly?”, “Here's the thing”, or “说实话” can delay a routine point.
Keep them when they convey an actual change of stance or the writer's natural speech.
Do not add a confession or personal struggle in their place.

### 34. Unraised objections

Check whether a disclaimer contributes a scientific boundary, real correction,
counterargument, or explicit position. Preserve those functions, even without a
named critic. Remove only empty defensive staging. “This does not establish
causation” must survive when the evidence supports association only.

### 35. Rejected alternatives

Keep alternatives that express a real choice, constraint, or consequence. A claim
that restarting a service drops every active session is substantive even if its
option seems unwise. Rewrite the relation directly if needed; do not silently
delete the consequence as a “fake option”. An empty imagined alternative can be cut.
